Opening Note
From the Desk of MAY INT Intelligence

The external accounts remain sound but are losing momentum. Exports rose 4.4% year-on-year in October to about $3.99 billion, with ready-made garments up about 4.9% to $3.17 billion, a sharp slowdown from the 13.1% total export growth recorded in August. Remittances were $2.87 billion in October, up 12.1% (August: 22.3%), taking July–October to $11.47 billion, up 13%. Bangladesh Bank's reserves at the end of October were $36.33 billion gross and $31.46 billion under BPM6, about $1 billion below August but 12% and 14% above a year earlier. The taka was quoted at 124.59 on Bangladesh Bank's November 15 rate sheet, about 1.5% weaker than the 122.74 we reported in September.

Inflation is flat but changing character. Headline CPI was 8.27% in October (September: 8.34%). Food inflation fell to 6.40%, a 54-month low, while non-food inflation rose to 9.89%, its highest in 41 months. The wage rate index rose 7.63%, below headline inflation.

The larger story is financing. Private sector credit growth fell to 4.17% in September, the lowest since the series began in 2003, even though Bangladesh Bank cut its policy rate by 50 basis points to 9.5% on July 30. The central bank says banks are placing surplus funds in government securities, and the government had borrowed about 43% of its full-year bank-borrowing target within four months. The National Board of Revenue's shortfall against target passed Tk 50,000 crore for July–October, and the World Bank's October update projects growth of just 3.4% in FY27. In MAY INT's reading these facts are linked: a revenue gap raises government borrowing from banks, and banks earning a return on government paper have less reason to lend to private firms. The Insight of the Month develops this.

Two items we have been tracking moved. On October 29 the UN General Assembly approved a three-year deferral of Bangladesh's LDC graduation to November 24, 2029, so the November 24, 2026 date no longer applies. A new IMF programme has still not been agreed. We also found that our August and September issues gave the policy rate as 10% when it had already been cut to 9.5%; see the corrections in Regulatory Watch.

MAY INT's read for November: Bangladesh's external position is a cushion, not a growth engine. Dollar inflows are healthy but decelerating, while the domestic channels that turn them into growth, bank credit, government revenue and reliable energy, are all under strain. Shipping and Strait of Hormuz developments are covered in the Maritime section.

Economic Outlook
Cushion Intact, Growth Engine Stalling

The World Bank's October 6 Bangladesh Development Update projects growth of 3.4% in FY26 and FY27, rising to 3.9% in FY28, and calls for urgent financial sector, energy and revenue reforms. Press coverage describes the FY27 figure as 1.2 percentage points below the Bank's earlier projection. It is close to the 3.5% IMF figure we reported in July and well below the 6.5% growth target Bangladesh Bank cited in its July policy statement.

The external accounts explain why the balance of payments is not the binding constraint. Reserves of $31.46 billion (BPM6) are 14% above a year earlier, remittances are running 13% ahead for the fiscal year so far, and exports are growing. But the pace has slowed in both: remittance growth of 12.1% in October compares with 22.3% in August, and export growth of 4.4% compares with 13.1%. Reserves are also about $1 billion lower than at the end of August. We do not have a complete import picture for September–October, so we cannot say how much of the reserve decline reflects import payments and how much reflects other flows.

Inflation: Cost Pressures Are Shifting

Headline inflation has been close to 8.3% for three months, but the mix has changed. Food inflation has fallen to 6.40%, helped by lower global wheat prices (see Commodities). Non-food inflation rose to 9.89%. On a month-on-month basis consumer prices rose 1.82% in October, up from 1.12% in September. The wage rate index grew 7.63%, below headline inflation, which on this measure implies a small decline in real wages. Inflation remains above the 7.5% ceiling in the FY27 budget.

Energy supply is still rationed. Press reports put national gas demand at about 4.0 bcfd against a shortfall in supply, with Petrobangla rationing among industry, power and other users. In early November the Summit floating LNG terminal was taken offline for repairs, and Petrobangla said regasified LNG to the grid would fall to 600 mmcfd from 920 mmcfd, with possible low pressure in parts of the country. Petrobangla also said the nine LNG cargoes needed for November were confirmed, but a Financial Express report says November import volume is about 10% below October's with no spot purchases planned, on the expectation that winter lowers demand. We did not find reports on factory-level gas cuts in November, so we cannot say how far the latest outage has reached industry.
Trade Finance
A Weaker Taka Moves Into LC Costs

The most direct trade finance development this month is the exchange rate. Bangladesh Bank's weighted average rate moved from 122.74 on September 21 to 124.59 on November 15, so each dollar of import payment costs about Tk 1.85 (1.5%) more than two months ago. A one-off move of this size is modest, but importers settling usance LCs opened at the earlier rate face it as an unbudgeted cost, and exporters with dollar receipts and taka costs benefit. The Case Study works through an example.

Practical Points for the Month

FX exposure on open LCs. Banks should check which importers hold open usance LCs without forward cover, particularly those in cotton, fuel and other dollar-priced inputs, and ask whether their pricing assumed the earlier rate. We do not know why the taka has weakened and do not forecast further moves.

LNG payments. Asian LNG (JKM) is $31.35 per mmbtu, up 14% since mid-September and about 182% above a year ago. September cargoes were contracted at $22 to $24.62, so the November price environment is meaningfully higher than those reference points. We do not have the pricing of the nine confirmed November cargoes. Banks handling sovereign energy import payments should size exposure against current benchmarks rather than September contract prices, and MAY INT continues to recommend buffers of 20% to 25% above spot on any energy-linked LC with a tenor beyond 45 days.

Freight. Container rates are easing, with the Drewry index at $4,305 per 40ft on November 12, but carriers have announced higher FAK and peak-season surcharges on Asia–Europe routes for the second half of November. Exporters and buyers agreeing delivered-price terms should confirm whether surcharges are for the shipper's account.

Shipment dates still matter. Our September Case Study on LC shipment-date risk for gas-constrained exporters remains relevant while gas supply stays rationed. Amendment routes agreed early are cheaper than discrepancies discovered after a missed date.
Credit & Counterparty Risk
Bank Balance Sheets Are the Weak Point

Banking sector. The World Bank update reports, citing Bangladesh Bank data as carried in the press, that the non-performing loan ratio rose to 33.2% in June 2026 from 20.2% at the end of December 2024. We have not seen the underlying table, so treat the figure as reported. It is consistent with the other evidence: private credit growth at a record low, banks buying government securities, and a lending-spread cap that Bangladesh Bank reinstated in June (4%), which the World Bank wants removed. For credit teams, the practical inference is that counterparty banks are conserving capital and liquidity. Confirming lines and facilities from Bangladeshi banks deserve closer review of capital adequacy, not only country risk.

Sovereign. The positives are reserves 12% to 14% above a year ago, steady remittances, and the LDC deferral, which removes a near-term preference cliff. The negatives are a revenue shortfall exceeding Tk 50,000 crore after four months, growth of about 3.4%, and no IMF anchor: a new programme is under negotiation but unagreed. MAY INT's assessment remains stable, not improving.

Corporate. Export order books are growing, but at a slower pace, and non-food cost inflation of 9.89% squeezes manufacturers' margins. Exporters themselves cited rising production costs, fuel shortages, banking difficulties and industrial unrest as risks to growth, according to press coverage of the October export data. Credit assessments should ask about each of these directly.

Maritime & Supply Chain
Hormuz Counts Disagree; Freight Rates Drift Lower

Reports of traffic through the Strait of Hormuz in mid-November differ widely, mostly because sources count different things. IMF PortWatch logged 3 transits on November 8 against a pre-crisis baseline of about 85 a day. Windward counted 9 tankers and cargo vessels on November 13, nearly all with AIS transponders off. A UKMTO weekly report counted 170 outbound and 155 inbound transits as of November 10; the reporting period is not clear from the coverage we saw. United Against Nuclear Iran, an advocacy group, tracks US-facilitated southern-corridor transits and reports an average of 33.8 a day, with a record 135 in the 96 hours to November 11. That series measures US-coordinated passage, not total traffic.

How to read this: Counts that include escorted or dark-fleet traffic are higher than counts of visible commercial movements. MAY INT treats the PortWatch figure as the best gauge of normal commercial shipping, which remains far below baseline. Separate reports include an unverified claim by Iran's Revolutionary Guard of a mine strike on a gas carrier on November 13, and a US blockade of Iranian ports since July.

Container Freight

Drewry's World Container Index fell 1% to $4,389 per 40ft on November 5 and 2% to $4,305 on November 12, with demand described as weak. Shanghai to New York fell 3% to $9,020, the lowest since August; Shanghai to Los Angeles was $7,416, Rotterdam $3,504 and Genoa $4,168. Congestion at Shanghai port rose to 49 hours from 36, and Drewry expects a further decline next week (next release: November 19).

For Bangladesh, which buys LNG for power and industry, the LNG price matters more than Brent.

Regulatory & Compliance Watch
Policy Rate Correction, LDC Decision, and an Unagreed IMF Programme
Corrections and clarifications to earlier issues.
1. Policy rate (August and September issues). Both issues stated the policy rate was 10%. Bangladesh Bank cut it by 50 basis points to 9.5% on July 30, effective August 2 — the first cut in six years — and held it at 9.5% in its first quarterly policy statement on September 30. The Standing Lending Facility rate is 11% and the Standing Deposit Facility rate 7.5%. The September issue's statement that the rate had been 10% since October 2024 and its table row "10.00% Unchanged" should be disregarded, and its discussion of the cost of credit should be read against a 9.5% rate. (Our July issue, published July 26, correctly reported 10% at the time.)
2. Rice price (September issue). The rice figure we quoted is a US rough rice futures price, not a Bangladesh or Asian export price.
3. Status of September's corrections. The LDC item is now resolved (below). On the IMF, the programme exit announced June 3 and a request for a new programme stand; no new agreement has been reached.

LDC Graduation Deferred to 2029

The UN General Assembly adopted resolution A/81/L.8 on October 29 (New York time), extending Bangladesh's graduation date to November 24, 2029, with Nepal receiving the same extension. One report describes the approval as unanimous. A member of the UN Committee for Development Policy was quoted as saying there will be no further extension beyond 2029. The decision keeps current preferences for three more years; it does not change what follows them.

New IMF Programme: No Agreement Yet

The most recent report we found says Bangladesh is negotiating a replacement for its previous programme and targets a staff-level agreement by the end of 2026, with the size and instalments to be settled in negotiations and at the IMF board. We treat that target as unconfirmed. Press reports say the move to quarterly monetary policy statements followed an IMF condition.

Quarterly Monetary Policy and the Spread Cap

Bangladesh Bank issued its first quarterly statement for October–December on September 30, holding the policy rate at 9.5%. It cited inflation risks, noting non-food inflation of 9.32% at the time, and acknowledged that high borrowing costs are holding back private credit. The 4% cap on lending spreads, reinstated in June, remains in place; the World Bank has recommended removing it.

Revenue Administration

NBR reinstated monthly VAT return filing at the start of October after officials linked part of the VAT shortfall to the earlier filing change. It has also launched sector-specific collection drives and is reported to be approaching retailers to help cover the shortfall.

Budget Watch
Four Months In, the Revenue Gap Is Widening

In September we found no published FY27 collection data. We now have preliminary figures. For July–October the NBR's target was nearly Tk 178,000 crore and collections were below Tk 128,000 crore, a shortfall above Tk 50,000 crore. After the first quarter the shortfall was about Tk 42,000 crore, so it widened in October. Collections rose by less than 6% year-on-year; the full-year target of Tk 604,000 crore requires growth of more than 45%.

VAT is the weakest line, with a deficit of Tk 20,057 crore against its target, and officials partly attribute it to the return-filing change that has since been reversed. Even if VAT recovers, the arithmetic is difficult: growth of about 14% in income and import taxes, if sustained, does not close a gap that requires 45% overall.

The financing link: A revenue gap has to be financed. Bangladesh Bank's report that the government had used about 43% of its Tk 1.12 lakh crore bank-borrowing target (roughly Tk 48,000 crore) within four months suggests the gap is already being met largely from the domestic banking system. That is the mechanism we flagged in September as one plausible contributor to weak private credit.

MAY INT's assessment: Our base case, a material FY27 shortfall in the range of FY26's Tk 87,527 crore or larger, is tracking. Without a mid-year revenue measure or expenditure adjustment, expect continued heavy bank borrowing and little room for private credit to recover. A caveat: the data come from press reports of preliminary NBR estimates, and a recovery in VAT would change the picture.

Opportunity Corner
Three Openings for Banks and Traders
FX Risk Management
Hedging Products for Importers Facing a Weaker Taka
A 1.5% move in two months is a reminder that importers settling in dollars carry exchange-rate risk they may not have priced. Banks that can offer clear, affordable forward cover tied to LC settlement dates, and explain it in plain terms to mid-sized importers, can deepen relationships while reducing their own credit risk from surprise cost increases. Exporters with dollar receipts can be offered structures that net their exposure against importer clients' needs.
Non-Traditional Export Markets
Finance Growth Outside the EU and US
Press reports say RMG exports to non-traditional markets rose about 9% to $559.7 million in October, while shipments to the EU rose about 1.1% to $1.53 billion. The faster-growing segment is smaller, but it is where documentary practice, buyer credit checks and payment terms differ most from established EU and US business. Banks that build that expertise now will be positioned for the 2029 graduation date.
Working Capital for Gas-Exposed Manufacturers
Fund Resilience, Not Only Orders
With gas rationing continuing and credit scarce, manufacturers that invest in captive power, rooftop solar or dual-fuel equipment are making a case that is easier to underwrite than a general working-capital line: the equipment has a defined cost, a measurable output and a clear operational purpose. Equipment import LCs and term finance for this purpose deserve dedicated product design.

Insight of the Month
Credit Update: The Evidence Is Pointing at Banks and the Government

Last month we asked why record dollar inflows were not reaching the credit market, and offered four hypotheses: the cost of credit, bank risk aversion, weak borrower demand, and government borrowing. New data this month make some of them look stronger than others.

What Has Changed

The price of money does not look like the binding constraint. The policy rate was cut by 50 basis points to 9.5% effective August 2. Private credit growth then fell, from 4.75% in August to 4.17% in September. A rate cut that is followed by weaker credit growth is not what a cost-of-credit explanation would predict, though the cut is recent and transmission can lag. (Our September issue wrongly described the rate as 10%; the hypothesis stands but the rate was already lower.)

Banks are choosing government paper. Bangladesh Bank itself says banks are increasingly putting surplus funds into government securities rather than lending to the private sector. This is a statement of observed behaviour from the regulator, not an inference of ours.

The government is borrowing heavily. About 43% of the Tk 1.12 lakh crore domestic bank-borrowing target was used in the first four months of the fiscal year, while the revenue shortfall widened beyond Tk 50,000 crore. The World Bank warns that heavy government borrowing from banks could tighten credit to private firms.

Bank balance sheets are weak. The reported non-performing loan ratio of 33.2% in June, and a lending-spread cap that commentators say limits banks' ability to price risk, are both consistent with banks preferring safe government assets to uncertain private borrowers.

What Remains Unresolved

We still cannot separate bank unwillingness from borrower weakness. Gas rationing, slowing export growth and rising non-food costs all reduce borrowers' appetite for new debt. The three series that would help are Bangladesh Bank's monthly credit release for October, banks' holdings of government securities, and the pace of non-performing loan recognition.

MAY INT's assessment: The most consistent reading of the evidence is that private credit is being squeezed from the supply side: a weak banking system and a government that needs to borrow. That implies that a further rate cut alone is unlikely to revive lending, and that progress depends on bank resolution and on closing the revenue gap. We hold this view with moderate confidence; the next monthly credit figure will test it.

Case Study
A Cotton Importer Meets a Weaker Taka

This is an illustrative composite, not a named client. Consider a Dhaka spinner importing raw cotton under a 90-day usance LC for US$1,000,000. The importer priced the purchase when the Bangladesh Bank reference rate was about Tk 122.74 per dollar (September 21) and did not buy forward cover. At settlement the reference rate is Tk 124.59 (November 15). We use these two reference rates for illustration only; actual bank rates for customers differ.

Separately, cotton is 31.4% above a year ago in dollar terms (about $1.81 per kg), so the same dollar amount buys roughly 24% less cotton than a year earlier. The exchange rate cost is on top of the commodity price effect, not part of it.

What the Bank and Importer Can Do

Forward cover. If the importer had bought a forward contract at opening, the taka cost would have been fixed (the forward rate would have included a premium; we do not have the market's forward rates). Whether to hedge is a business judgment, but a known dollar liability on a known date is the textbook case.

Margin and facility sizing. A bank that sets its taka margin requirement and facility limit on the opening rate should ask whether a 2–3% adverse move would breach limits or push the borrower into a cash shortfall at settlement.

Natural offsets. Borrowers that also export, such as vertically integrated garment groups, hold dollar receipts that offset some of the exposure. Credit files should record this so that the bank evaluates net, not gross, exposure.

Pricing pass-through. Spinners that sell yarn in taka bear the full effect unless contracts allow price adjustment. Ask whether sales contracts contain exchange-rate or raw-material clauses.

The lesson is a modest one. A 1.5% move is not a crisis, but across a portfolio of importers with unhedged dollar liabilities it adds up, and it arrives when margins are already under pressure from cotton prices and non-food inflation.

Thought Leadership
Three Reforms, One Scorecard: Reading the World Bank's List Against This Month's Data

The World Bank's October update says Bangladesh needs urgent financial sector, energy and revenue reforms to restore growth and create jobs. It is a short list, and this month's data happen to give one reading for each.

They Are Not Independent

Each area feeds the others. A revenue gap raises government borrowing from banks, which crowds out private credit. Weak private credit limits the investment, including in captive power and more efficient energy use, that would reduce reliance on imported LNG. Energy shortages reduce output and hence the tax base. A reform sequence that treats them separately risks fixing one while the others pull it back.

A View on Sequencing

MAY INT's view, offered as opinion not finding, is that revenue and bank resolution are the two that unlock the third. Energy investment needs bank finance, and bank finance for it competes with the government for the same deposits. If the revenue gap narrowed and bank resolution progressed, credit for energy and productive investment would have room to grow. If neither moved, even a good energy plan would struggle to find funding. The deferral of LDC graduation to 2029 gives a time window, but a bounded one: the UN committee member quoted in the press said not to expect another extension.

Where an IMF programme fits: A new programme, if agreed, would likely bundle conditions on all three areas, which is a reason the negotiation matters beyond the disbursement itself. Its absence leaves Bangladesh without an external anchor for the reform sequence. We do not forecast the timing of an agreement.

MAY INT's assessment: The data this month are not a reason for alarm; the external accounts are sound. They are a reason to judge policy by the three domestic indicators above, because those, more than the dollar inflows, will decide whether the economy grows closer to 3.4% or to the 6.5% its central bank has targeted.
